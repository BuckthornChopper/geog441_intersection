Literature Review

Several papers and sources were helpful to my thinking about town centers, TIFs, and early Mequon planning.

A mixed-use renaissance: a renewed interest in creating a vibrant urban feel in city centers and in suburban settings is propelling successful mixed-use development. Here are three projects that blend the best of mixed use

Bell, Mortgage Banking (2004)

In Mortgage Banking in 2004, John Bell wrote that mixed-use developments are “the heavyweight champions of commercial real estate.”

He cites the Urban Land Institute’s definition of a mixed-use development as having three characteristics:

  • Three or more significant revenue-producing uses that are mutually supporting in well-planned projects;
  • A relatively close-knit use of land with integration of project components, including uninterrupted pedestrian connections; and
  • Development in conformance with a coherent plan that often stipulates the type and scale of uses, permitted densities, and related items.

He explains the upswing in mixed-use developments due to several factors:

  • Referring to John McIlwain from the Urban Land Institute, an increased interest in urbanization or urban-like environments;
  • Baby Boomers entering their empty-nest phase looking for urban convenience “whether it’s downtown or in the suburbs;”
  • Suburbanites who want an urban experience but want to remain in the suburbs.

Bell cites Herman Bulls, of Public Institutions in DC, outlining a blueprint for mixed-use development:

  • Market dynamics
  • Enlightened local government
  • Availability of capital
  • Favorable traffic patterns in the development
  • Sufficient parking
  • Cross-easements if the development has multiple owners

He concludes with a survey of three successful mixed-use developments:

  • Reston Town Center, suburban Washington
  • Water Tower Place, Chicago
  • Peabody Place, Memphis

Suburban mixed-use centres and urban dispersion: what difference do they make?

Filion, Environment and Planning A: Economy and Space (2001)

Filion, in a paper published in 2001 in Environment and Planning A: Economy and Space, described his analysis of three mixed-use developments in Greater Toronto. He explains that these mixed-use developments were built in alignment with ‘metro-wide planning objectives.’ Two interlocking planning objectives were to increase the intensity of urban development and to reduce the reliance on cars. Toronto has significant mass transit.

He wrote that the developments had the potential to break the traditional suburban morphology. He said that mixed-use developments ‘...shares the general drift of the definition of edge cities Garreau proposed some ten years ago.’ in 1991.

Filion criticizes aspects of the three developments…

  • unappealing pedestrian environments
  • excess surface area devoted to cars compared with building footprints.

He uses the term ‘inner synergy’ to describe how self-sufficient a development is. He gathered data about workers’ restaurant choices within and beyond the development and their use of cars to travel within the development. He reported that the data indicated that the developments were falling short of their planned goals.

However, he notes that the developments’ densities far exceed suburban norms. They were distinguished from the suburban environment more by their density than by their inner synergy. He concludes with a useful summary: ‘To a large extent, it is then the reverberation of features of dispersed urbanization on suburban mixed-use centres that thwarts their adoption of a highly distinctive urban form and land-use dynamic.’

If You Promise to Build It, Will They Come? The Interaction between Local Economic Development Policy and the Real Estate Market: Evidence from Tax Increment Finance Districts

Smith, Real Estate Economics (2009)

Smith, writing in Real Estate Economics in 2009, describes a carefully-controlled analysis that he did of the impact that TIFs have on the value of land and commercial properties. TIFs were developed in California in 1952 and are used in all states except Arizona. The City of Chicago is a major user of the idea with, at the time of writing, 131 TIFs with total tax receipts of $325 million per year, about a third of the city’s total property tax revenue.

Through careful study design, he overcame a number of deficits of prior analyses. He began with two hypotheses…

  • properties in a TIF will have a faster rate of appreciation than those outside the district
  • the rate of change of appreciation will be faster in the district after the area’s been designated as a TIF.

He notes that those who support the idea of TIFs argue that outside investment creates jobs and adds to the tax base. Those who denigrate the idea claim that investment is merely shifted from one jurisdiction in a city to another, the investment would have been made without the TIF.

Smith concludes that…

  • TIFs face increasing scrutiny by elected officials and citizens
  • they’re not without cost, they can freeze a portion of a city’s tax base while demands for services grow
  • TIFs and their resulting investments raise the prices of commercial properties and the value of the tax base.
